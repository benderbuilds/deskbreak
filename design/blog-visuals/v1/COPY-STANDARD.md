# DeskBreak writing standard

Use this standard for article text, figures, captions, page summaries and calls to action. Version 1.1, September 25, 2026.

## Who we are writing for

Adults who work at a computer and want a useful answer during a busy day. Assume no background in medicine or research. Readers may be tired, scanning on a phone, using assistive technology, or reading English as an additional language. Write respectfully; simple language does not mean talking down to people.

Our job is to help readers understand a finding, its practical relevance and its limits. A research diagram does not need a sales message or an exercise recommendation.

## The article pattern

1. **Answer the reader's question first.** Use two or three short sentences. State uncertainty in that answer when it changes the meaning.
2. **Explain the finding.** Say who took part, what people did, what was compared and what was measured. Introduce a study only after the reader knows why it matters.
3. **Show it.** Use one figure for one idea, placed beside the relevant explanation. Give it a useful title and a visible caption and source link.
4. **Explain the limit.** Keep the key qualification beside the finding. More detail can sit in an ordinary disclosure that readers can open.
5. **Offer a practical next step.** Label a suggestion as a suggestion. A schedule used in an experiment is not automatically advice for everyone.
6. **Offer one relevant action.** Use specific wording such as “Try a 3-minute desk break.” Match the actual destination and duration. Avoid vague “Learn more” buttons or promises the product has not demonstrated.

## Sentence and word choices

- Prefer familiar words and active sentences: “Researchers compared three groups.”
- Aim for one idea per sentence. Split sentences that combine a finding, a qualification and a recommendation. Sentence length is an editing signal, not a rigid score.
- Use short paragraphs and headings that tell readers what they will learn. Cut throat-clearing, rhetorical filler and repeated disclaimers.
- Use numbers where they make a comparison easier. Name the unit, time point and comparison: “22 fewer minutes per day than the group with usual work habits, after 12 months.”
- Keep necessary technical terms in the detailed explanation, with an immediate definition. Do not remove the method or its uncertainty just to make the copy shorter.
- Use American English for newly authored explanatory copy: program, behavior, meters. Preserve official study titles, proper names and verbatim quotations.
- Avoid absolute claims such as “nobody has tested,” “proven,” “always” or “everyone” unless the supporting scope genuinely allows them. Prefer “This study did not test...”
- Distinguish what people reported from what researchers measured. Avoid turning an uncontrolled observation into a cause-and-effect claim.
- Use calm, useful language. Avoid fear about sitting, guilt about missed breaks, fake urgency and promises of pain relief or corrected posture.

| Instead of | Use |
| --- | --- |
| Symptomatic computer users | Computer users with eye strain |
| A familiar mnemonic | An easy way to remember a screen break |
| Baseline monitoring | Before reminders |
| Workplace clusters | Workplace groups; explain that groups were assigned by chance |
| Post-drink glucose | Blood sugar after a test drink |
| Accommodative facility | A focusing test; define the technical term in the detail |
| Whiskers show 95% CI | Dots show estimates. Bars show uncertainty; explain the statistical range in the detail |
| Adjusted between-group differences | A comparison between groups that accounts for factors such as starting sitting time |

## Visual and number rules

- Readers should understand the main point from the title, figure and caption together, without opening the source paper.
- Use the mobile composition at phone sizes. Keep the comparison, time frame, units and essential qualification visible. Do not shrink the desktop chart to make it fit.
- Color reinforces meaning; labels must carry it too. Keep text readable against its background.
- Remove decoration that looks like data. A row of arbitrary blocks must not resemble a count of study participants or walking breaks.
- Define chart marks. An uncertainty interval is not the range of individual experiences or a guarantee.
- Round numbers only when the prose clearly says “about.” Preserve exact values in the chart and accessible table.
- Alt text identifies the finding or purpose. Captions explain the takeaway. Longer HTML descriptions and tables provide the full detail. Do not force all three jobs into alt text.
- Sources stay visible beside captions. Do not put the only source link or key limitation behind a disclosure.

## Reader check before scaling the catalog

Editorial inspection can find unclear writing; it cannot prove everyone understands it. Recruit a small first group of desk workers with different reading confidence, screen sizes and accessibility needs. Five readers is a practical first round proposed for this project, not a certification or a statistically representative sample. No invitations have been sent.

Show each person an article or figure without coaching. Ask:

1. What is the main point, in your own words?
2. Who was studied, and what did they do?
3. What do the numbers compare?
4. What does the study leave unanswered?
5. What would you do next, if anything?

Watch for the specific misunderstandings this package must prevent: 20 minutes interpreted as an official universal rule; a desk alone credited with the combined program's result; uncertainty bars read as individual outcomes; reported symptom changes read as proven treatment. Revise confusing passages and test them again. Check the real article with keyboard navigation, zoom and a screen reader during implementation.

**PASS:** Editorial review of the six revised figures, captions and text equivalents against this standard. **NOT RUN:** Reader comprehension testing, independent CDC Index scoring, translation review and an assistive-technology audit. No universal readability or WCAG conformance claim is made.

## Guidance used

This house standard draws on the [CDC's Clear Communication Index guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/ccindex/tool/how-to-use.html) and [NIOSH's plain-language and design guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/bulletin/2017/clear-communication.html). They support making the main message easy to find, familiar language, clear visual explanations and audience testing. This document is our working standard, not a CDC endorsement or completed Index assessment.
